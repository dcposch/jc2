1      Introduction
Let K be a compact connected Lie group with normalized Haar measure dg. Mathieu conjectured
that if f and g are complex-valued K-finite functions and

for all sufficiently large n [6]. Duistermaat and van der Kallen proved the conjecture for compact
connected abelian groups [4]. Dings and Koelink studied the first nonabelian case K = SU (2),
proving the conjecture for several restricted classes and reducing the general problem to a
support conjecture for matrix coefficients [2].
    Müger and Tuset subsequently gave an abelian reduction of the SU (2) problem [7]. For
integers k, l ≥ 0, an admissible function is a polynomial

where each torus is equipped with normalized Haar measure. The cases l = 0 and k = 0 were
known, while Müger and Tuset recorded that, to the best of their knowledge, every genuinely
mixed case was open, including k = l = 1 [7, Remark 2.4]. They also proved that the case
(k, l) = (2, 1) would imply the Mathieu conjecture for SU (2) [7, Theorem 2.5].
     Mathieu proved that his conjecture for all compact connected Lie groups implies Keller’s
Jacobian Conjecture [6, 5]. On July 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge announced an explicit polynomial
map in three variables with constant nonzero Jacobian determinant and a fiber containing
three distinct points [1]. The determinant and collision identities are direct algebraic checks,
so the map disproves the Jacobian Conjecture in dimension three and, by adjoining identity
coordinates, in every dimension at least three. It follows that Mathieu’s conjecture cannot hold
for all compact connected Lie groups. This argument does not determine whether the conjecture
holds for SU (2), nor does it decide the separate xz(1, 1) problem. No part of the proofs below
depends on the Jacobian counterexample. The examples below settle both questions directly.

Remark 4.3. Mathieu’s implication from his compact-group conjecture to the Jacobian Conjecture
is one-way. Consequently, Theorem 4.2 does not by itself imply that the two-dimensional Jacobian
Conjecture is false.
    Taking λ = µ = 1 gives the particularly small pair announced in the abstract:

                                F = (1 + c)(ad + b),               G = −c.

The failure of eventual vanishing is maximal in the sense that the mixed moment is nonzero for
every n ≥ 1.


5     The cancellation mechanism
The counterexamples arise from an exact interaction between the binomial expansion and the
beta integral. For each 0 ≤ k ≤ n,
                                     !Z
                                 n         1                         1
                                               xk (1 − x)n−k dx =       .
                                 k     0                            n+1

Thus integrating the Bernstein expansion of ((1 − x) + xz)n removes the binomial coefficient
completely and leaves the geometric sum
                                       1
                                          (1 + z + · · · + z n ).
                                      n+1

The factor (1 − z −1 )n then contributes the alternating binomial row. The pure moment is
the full sum (1 − 1)n , while multiplication by z −1 removes the final term and leaves (−1)n−1 .
This explains both the all-moment cancellation and the persistent mixed moments without any
limiting argument.
    The examples were found during work on the Gaussian Moments Conjecture of Derksen, van
den Essen, and Zhao [3]. The contrast is instructive: Gaussian radial moments carry factorial
weights, whereas the beta integral in (3) exactly cancels the multinomial coefficient and permits
the alternating cancellation above. No result concerning Gaussian moments is needed for the
proofs in this paper.
