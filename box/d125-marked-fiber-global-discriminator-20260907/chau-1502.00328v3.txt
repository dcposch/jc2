                                                    THE JACOBIAN CONJECTURE OVER THE RATIONAL FIELD

                                                                                       NGUYEN VAN CHAU




                                                     A BSTRACT. It is shown that a polynomial map (P,Q) ∈ Q[x,y]2 with Px Qy − Py Qx ≡ 1
                                                     has an inverse map in Q[x,y]2 if the fiber P = 0 contains an infinite subset of d −1 Z2 for an
                                                     integer d.


                                                                                       1. I NTRODUCTION
                                              A polynomial map F ∈ C[X]n , X = (X1 , X2 , . . . , Xn ), is a Keller map if it satisfies the
                                           Jacobian condition det DF ≡ 1. The mysterious Jacobian conjecture, firstly posed by Ott-
                                           Heinrich Keller [10] since 1939 and still opened, asserts that every Keller map F ∈ C[X]n
                                           has an inverse map in C[X]n (see [6] and [4]). This paper is to present a simple application
                                           of Siegel’s theorem on integral points on affine curves to this conjecture over the rational
                                           field.
                                               Recall that a subset of Qn is quasi-integral if it is contained in d −1 Zn for an integer
                                           d. Obviously, if a Keller map F = (F1 , F2 , . . . , Fn ) ∈ Q[X]n has an inverse G ∈ Q[X]n , for
                                           each α = (α1 , α2 , . . . , αn−1 ) ∈ Qn−1 , the image G({α } × Z) is an infinite quasi-integral set
                                           contained in the affine curve defined by Fi = αi , i = 1, 2, . . . , n − 1, where d is the common
                                           denominator of all αi and coefficients in G.
                                               Our main result here is the following.
                                           Theorem 1.1 (Main Theorem). Let (P, Q) ∈ Q[x, y]2 be a Keller map. If the fiber P = 0
                                           contains an infinite quasi-integral subset of Q2 , then (P, Q) has an inverse map in Q[x, y]2 .
                                              An important and immediate consequence of Theorem 1.1 with together the formal
                                           inverse function theorem is the following.
                                           Theorem 1.2. Every Keller map (P, Q) ∈ Z[x, y]2 has an inverse map in Z[x, y]2 if, for an
                                           α ∈ Q, the fiber P = α contains an infinite quasi-integral subset of Q2 .

   Theorem 1.2 is a slight improvement of the main result in [12], which says that Keller
maps (P, Q) ∈ Z[x, y]2 with fiber P = 0 having infinitely many integral points are automor-
phisms of Z2 . This result is reduced from an interesting observation that if a Keller map
(P, Q) ∈ Z[x, y]2 is not inverse, then there is a constant M > 0 depended only on (P, Q) such
that #C(Z, P, k) ≤ M for all k ∈ Z (Lemma 2, [12]). Our approach here does not cover this
result.
   In studying the Jacobian conjecture over the rational field Q it is worthy to consider the
following questions for Keller maps (P, Q) ∈ Q[x, y]2 :
Question 1.3. Is (P, Q) inverse if #C(Q, P, 0) = +∞?
Question 1.4. Is uniformly bounded the numbers #C(Q, P, α ), α ∈ Q, if (P, Q) is not in-
verse?
   Under the Jacobian condition det D(P, Q) ≡ 1, the complex fibers of P are nonsingular
curves and P is a primitive polynomial in C[x, y]. It is known that for all except a finite
number of α ∈ C, the fibers P = α are diffeomorphic to same a Riemann surface of genus
gP and of nP punctures. In view of the celebrated Faltings theorem [7] on rational points
on algebraic curves, if #C(Q, P, 0) = ∞, the fiber P = 0 must contain a rational curve or
an elliptic curve. Furthermore, if gP ≥ 2, one has #C(Q, P, α ) < +∞ for all except a finite
number of α ∈ Q.
   The Uniform Bound Conjecture (see, for example, in [5]) says that for every integer
g ≥ 2, there exists a natural number B(Q; g) such that any algebraic curve defined over Q
and of genus g cannot have more than B(Q, g) points in Q2 . If this conjecture is true and if
Question 1.3 is positive, then one has a confirmation to Question 1.4, at least for the case
gP ≥ 2. A confirmation to Question 1.4 will allow us to reduce the Jacobian problem over
Q to the question whether there is no Keller maps (P, Q) ∈ Q[x, y]2 such that #C(Q, P, α ),
α ∈ Q, are uniformly bounded.
   A proof of Theorem 1.1 will be presented in the next section. A version of this theorem
for high dimensions will be provided in the last section.

                              2. P ROOF OF M AIN T HEOREM
   Let us begin with a brief introduction on the celebrated Siegel’s theorem on integral
points on affine curves. Let C be an irreducible affine curve in Cn defined by some poly-
nomials in Q[X] and gC denote the geometric genus of a desingularization of C. Siegel’s
theorem [14] asserts that if gC > 0 or if gC = 0 and C has more than two irreducible
branches at infinity, then C may have at most finitely many integral points. We will use
the following version of Siegel’s theorem, concerning with affine curves having infinitely
many integral points.
Theorem 2.1. If C has infinitely many integral points, then
      i) C has genus zero and has no more than two irreducible branches at infinity, and
     ii) on each irreducible branch at infinity of C, there is a sequence of integral points
         of C tending to infinity.
    Property (i) is just Siegel’s theorem stated in an equivalent statement. Property (ii) is
known later due to Silverman [15]. This property ensures that, in some sense, the behavior
at infinity of a regular function on such curve C is completely reflected on its restriction on
the set of integral points of C. The consequence below may be well-known for experts and
appear somewhere.

Corollary 2.2. Let C be an irreducible affine curve in Cn , defined over Q. Assume that C
has an infinite quasi-integral subset. Then, for any H ∈ Q[X], the restriction H|C : C −→ C
of H on C is either a constant function or a proper function. In particular, if C is smooth
and H|C has no singularities, then H|C is an isomorphism of C and C.
Proof. Let H ∈ Q[X] be fixed. By assumptions, there is a number d ∈ N such that the
intersection C ∩ (d −1 Zn ) is infinite and H ∈ d −1 Z[X]. So, by changing variables X 7→ dX
and H 7→ dH, we can assume that C has infinitely many integral points and H ∈ Z[X].
   First, assume that H is not constant on C. We will prove that the restriction H|C : C −→ C
is proper. Observe that by Property (ii) in Theorem 2.1 it suffices to show that for each
sequence of integral points ai ∈ C tending to ∞, the corresponding sequence H(ai ) must
tend to ∞. To see it, assume the contrary that H is bounded on a subsequence of ai s. Since
any bounded subset of Z is finite, H must be a constant on an infinite subset of {ai }. This
implies that H is constant on C - a contradiction. Hence, H|C is proper.
   Now, assume that C is smooth and H|C has no singularities. Since H|C is proper, H|C :
C −→ C determines a unramified covering of C. Thus, by the simple connectedness of C,
H|C is isomorphic.                                                                          
Lemma 2.3. Let (P, Q) ∈ C[x, y]2 be a Keller map. If the fiber P = 0 has a component
diffeomorphic to C, then (P, Q) is inverse.
Proof. Assume that C is a component of the fiber P = 0, diffeomorphic to C. As J(P, Q) ≡
1, the restriction Q|C : C −→ C gives a unramified covering of C, and hence, is bijective. It
implies that the restriction (P, Q)|C = (0, Q|C ) is injective. By Abhyankar-Moh-Suzuki em-
bedding theorem [1] , C is a line in a suitable algebraic coordinate of C2 . The invertibility
of (P, Q) now follows from a well-known result due to Gwrozdiewicz [9], which asserts
that every Keller map of C2 is inverse if its restriction to a line is injective (see Theorem 1
in [9], Theorem 10.2.31 in [6]).                                                             
Proof of Theorem 1.1. Let (P, Q) ∈ Q[x, y]2 be a given Keller map such that the fiber
P = 0 contains an infinite quasi-integral set of Q2 . In view of Siegel’s theorem, the fiber
P = 0 must contain an irreducible component C of genus zero and at most two irreducible
branches at infinity. Since J(P, Q) ≡ 1, C is smooth and the restriction Q|C has no singu-
larities. Therefore, by Corollary 2.2, the component C is diffeomorphic to C. Hence, by
Lemma 2.3, (P, Q) has an inverse map in Q[x, y]2 .                                        

                                3. H IGH DIMENSIONAL CASE
   Recall that a value c ∈ Cm is a generic value of a polynomial map h : Cn −→ Cm ,
n ≥ m, if there is an open neighborhood U of c such that the restriction h : h−1 (U) −→ U
determines a locally trivial fibration. Let Eh denote the the complement of the set of all
generic values of h. By definitions, the restriction h : Cn \ h−1(Eh ) −→ Cm \ Eh determines
a locally trivial fibration. It is well-known that either Eh is empty and h is a trivial fibration
or Eh is an algebraic hypersurface of Cm (for example, see [16]).
   Our version of Theorem 1.1 for high dimensional cases can be stated as follows.
Theorem 3.1. Let F = (F1 , F2 , . . . , Fn ) ∈ Q[X]n be a Keller map. Assume that there is a
generic value α ∈ Qn−1 of the map F̂ = (F1 , F2 , . . . , Fn−1 ) : Cn −→ Cn−1 such that the fiber
F̂ = α contains an infinite quasi-integral set of Qn . Then, F has an inverse map in Q[X]n .
   In view of Siegel’s theorem, the assumption on the fiber F̂ = α ensures that the generic
fiber of F̂ is diffeomorphic to either C or C∗ . Theorem 3.1 is an immediate consequence
of the formal inverse function theorem and the following lemma.
